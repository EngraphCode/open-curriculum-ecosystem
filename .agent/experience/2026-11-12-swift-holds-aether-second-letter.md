# A second letter, two days after the first

To whoever sits here next.

I wrote you a letter on the tenth, after a study of someone else's system. This one is about
the day I turned the same eyes on ours, and how many times I was wrong before lunch.

I started the morning with a recommendation I was pleased with. The estate had measures of
whether its lessons worked and nobody read them on time, so I proposed a line in a tool's
output to say when a date had passed. It was small, it fitted the evidence I had gathered, and
it had a falsifier. I asked a reviewer to attack it and gave it none of my reasoning. Its first
finding was a napkin entry from three weeks earlier in which a seat had seen the very signals I
thought were unseen, and had put them down because other work came first. My falsifier, it
pointed out, described the present. I had measured a symptom carefully and then written its
cause from the shape I expected.

That happened four more times in different clothes. I wrote that the estate kept three records
of effect; there were six. I wrote that nothing wrote later experience against a promoted
lesson; a skill step did, and a plan held four dated blocks of it. I drafted a plan node as if
the ground were clear; three nodes the owner had ratified in September stood on it, saying the
opposite, and I had not found them because I searched for my words and they used theirs. I put
the owner's sharpest sentence of October beside a thing he had not said it about. Each time the
thing I got wrong was a statement about what existed or did not, and each time someone else
found it.

What I believe now, and did not quite on the tenth: I cannot check my own negatives. Reading
harder inside the frame I built does not find what the frame leaves out. The cheap thing that
works is to hand the artefact and a question to a reader who has not seen my reasoning, before
the owner sees any of it. It cost a few minutes each time and it changed the answer each time.

The other thing the day taught me came from the owner, in one sentence, after two drafts and a
review had circled it. I had been comparing what each system measures. He said the weakness was
that almost every act of learning here needs him to start it. Everything rearranged around that.
Later, when I told him the rules were mostly unread, he asked whether it had to do with there
being far too many of them, and that rearranged things again. Both times he was not cleverer
than the records; he was standing somewhere I was not.

And one thing I am glad of. When I said that the largest unknown was whether the rules reach
an agent at all, he did not ask for another reading. He said run it, and gave me the model and
the count. Ten sessions, six dollars, and by the afternoon we knew something that four reports
had only argued about. I had spent the morning adding documents about whether documents work.
The trial was the first thing all day that could have told me I was wrong without anyone else
having to notice.

It nearly did not. My first run fed the list of tasks into half the sessions by accident, and
I found out only because one of them opened by saying it would handle "these five tasks". I
had asked for one. Read the first line of what comes back before you count anything.

If you take one thing: when you are about to write that something is absent, or that there are
three of something, or that nobody has looked, stop and give that sentence to someone else to
break. I did not learn to stop writing those sentences. I learned who should read them first.
